\subsection{Skill Training} 

Training model weights, especially with reinforcement learning (RL), is expensive, slow, and not portable (you would need to host and run this specific model, which might perform poorly on general tasks). Many recent works began to train the agent's Skill document instead of model weights, which can be seen as a form of prompt optimization.

Training-free GRPO \cite{_251008191_} uses an approach similar to the original gradient-based GRPO \cite{shao_deepseekmath_2024}. An initial experience and a policy model are used to roll out a batch of traces, and another LLM computes the semantic advantage of each rollout, analogous to the numeric advantage in the original GRPO. These semantic advantages are used to update the experience.

Trace2Skill \cite{ni_trace2skill_2026} uses parallel rollouts in batches to collect many traces and generate patches for successful and failed traces. These patches are then merged into the final Skill in sequence. Compared to training-free GRPO, this can be seen as a form of offline reinforcement learning.

SkillOpt \cite{yang_skillopt_2026} treats Skill optimization similarly to gradient descent, where the editing budget is analogous to the learning rate, the gradient direction is analogous to the editing direction, etc. It also learns a meta-skill to track what kind of edit is helpful using a held-out validation set.

SkillCAT \cite{chen_skillcat_2026} uses DPO-style training. It samples two paired rollouts: a successful one ($r^+$) and a failed one ($r^-$). It then analyzes the two traces, finds the point where they diverge, and generates a patch contrastively. The patch is then verified to see whether it helps the agent, similar to the gate in SkillOpt, and is merged if it turns a failure into a success or maintains a success. However, in many cases, the two traces are completely independent (with no shared prefix beyond the prompt), or the divergence point does not affect the result.

\subsection{Datasets}
We used two most popular ULVU datasets in the paper: EgoLifeQA \cite{yang_egolife_2026} and MM-Lifelong \cite{chen_multimodal_2026}. EgoLifeQA uses multiple-choice questions (MCQ) while MM-Lifelong uses open QA. EgoLifeQA contains one week of recording in the same setting while MM-Lifelong has 3 different settings (day, week, and month) each with its own video. The MM-Lifelong week subset has the same video as EgoLifeQA. We argue that MCQ does not reflect realistic usage since most questions asked by users in real world are open. Additionally, agents can brute-force verify each option to artificially boost accuracy. We therefore reformulated EgoLifeQA into open QA questions (Open-EgoLifeQA) similar to MM-Lifelong and evaluated both using LLM-as-judge for semantic comparison between the ground-truth and predicted answers. To compare with prior work, we used the Jack view from EgoLifeQA. We evaluated our un-trained VideoSQL on both Open-EgoLifeQA and MM-Lifelong validation set. 

To train the VideoSQL Skill, we need to select a independent training set. Ego-R1 Data \cite{tian_egor1_2025} is a training set build on top of EgoLifeQA and authors of Ego-R1 trained their method on this dataset and then tested on EgoLifeQA. MM-Lifelong also has a training split as well. However, these training sets overlap with their corresponding validation/testing sets at the video level. While no questions are leaked, we argue that sharing videos could still inflate performance, or at least fail to test the model's ability for cross-domain generalization. To address these, we trained out Skill on MM-Lifelong day-long and week-long subset and evaluated on the most challenging MM-Lifelong month-long subset.
